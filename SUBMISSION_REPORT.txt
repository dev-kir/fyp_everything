ABSTRACT

The increased use of containerized applications in today's software deployment has
led to substantial benefits in scalability, flexibility, and performance. However, these
advantages are accompanied by some operation challenges, particularly when it comes to
container failures at runtime. In Docker Swarm configurations, failure recovery solutions
are usually reactive and rely on simple health checks and container restarts that only react
after the total failure of the service has occurred, which translates to Mean Time to Recovery
(MTTR) of more than 20 seconds and frequent service unavailability. This research
overcomes these shortcomings by the design, implementation and validation of
SwarmGuard, a rule-based proactive recovery mechanism that detects early warning signs
of failure and applies context-aware recovery strategies before total service degradation
occurs. The system uses lightweight threshold-based logic to monitor CPU, memory and
network I/O metrics with 7-9 millisecond alert latency. A novel network-aware classification
separates between node specific problems, which demand container migration, and cluster
wide traffic surges, which demand horizontal scaling. SwarmGuard consists of three main
components: a monitoring agent written in Python, a rule-based decision engine written in
Python and a recovery executor that applies zero-downtime corrective action calls through
Docker Swarm's built-in rolling update API. The system uses InfluxDB for the storage of
metrics and Grafana for real-time visualizations and keeps minimum CPU usage (less than
5% per node). Experimental validation on a five-node physical testbed with the use of
Raspberry Pi version 1.2B+ devices showed that the performance improvement is significant
compared to Docker Swarm's reactive baseline: proactive migration saw a 97.4% reduction
in the mean MTTR (from 23.10 seconds to 0.60 seconds) and 80% of tests showed zero
measurable downtime compared to 0% in the reactive baseline. The horizontal auto-scaling
mechanism was able to provide 6.6 seconds mean scale up latency with a cooldown
effectiveness of 100%. This research shows that rule-based proactive recovery can deliver
near zero downtime container orchestration without the complexity of machine learning to
provide practical reliability enhancements to small to medium enterprises managing Docker
Swarm clusters.

CHAPTER 1

INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background of Study
Containerization technology has transformed the software deployment process since
Docker was introduced in 2013. Containers take advantage of OS-level virtualization
using Linux kernel functionalities (namespaces, cgroups) and make it possible to deploy
applications in a lightweight manner with milliseconds of startup time as opposed to
minutes in the case of virtual machines (Queiroz et al, 2023). This efficiency has made
containerization the de facto standard for cloud native applications.

Microservices architecture helped in the growth of containers as organizations
became able to divide monolithic applications into stand-alone deployable services
(Velepucha & Flores, 2023). According to Cloud Native Computing Foundation's
survey for 2024, 93% of 750+ organizations use Kubernetes and 80% of them are in
production, up from 66% in 2023 (CNCF, 2024). Docker Swarm provides easier
orchestration for SMEs as it is integratively natively available in Docker and has less
operational overhead compared to Kubernetes (Purahong et al., 2023).

Container failures in production have a direct effect on revenue and user experience.
According to industry research, 90% of enterprises experience more than $300,000 for
every hour of downtime, and 41% of them reported $1-5 million for each hour of
downtime (Information Technology Intelligence Consulting, 2024). Research shows
100ms load time improvements increase conversion rates by 8.4% (Deloitte, 2020).
Studies show that users manage to stay on the task during short delays but report much
poorer subjective states such as high frustration levels and tension when they have to
wait for more than 7-10 seconds (Arapakis et al., 2021).
1

Container technology has become a cornerstone of modern day IT infrastructure.
According to Virtualization Info (2025), more than 90% of organizations are now using
(or looking at) containers, and the container infrastructure software market was set to
be $465.8 million in 2020 and nearly doubled to $944 million in 2024, which shows
how important container orchestration platforms are like Docker Swarm continues to
be.

Current orchestration platforms have reactive recovery with health check based
detection and container restarts. Default configurations demonstrate 40 second
detection times, which can be taken down to 3 seconds with aggressive tuning that
comes with the risk of false positives (Yang & Kim, 2020). Recovery windows of 2030 seconds leads to the error of http 502/503 and unavailability of service. SwarmGuard
overcomes these limitations with rule-based proactive recovery; resource stress can be
detected before failure and near-zero downtime achieved through preventive migration.

1.2 Problem Statement
Current Docker Swarm implementations have guaranteed downtime of containers in
the case of failure because of reactive recovery mechanisms. The recovery sequence,
health check detection (30-40s), termination, scheduling, image pull, and startup, leads
to a 20-30 seconds MTTR in ideal situations and rises to 60+ seconds in cases where
images need to be pulled (Yang & Kim, 2020). Users get to face some kind of errors
like a problem 502/503 and complete unavailability during this window.

This is a lot of down time that creates a lot of business impact. Over 90% of
enterprises contend with costs of over $300,000/hour. 41% of enterprises report costs
ranging from $1-5 million/hour (Information Technology Intelligence Consulting,
2024). Research shows 100ms load time improvements increase conversion rates by
8.4% (Deloitte, 2020). Mobile users are also leaving behind sites that take a long time
to load, with studies showing that for every 0.1 second improvement in mobile site
speed, conversion rates for retail sites go up by 8.4% (Deloitte, 2020).
2

Existing solutions do not remove downtime. Enhanced reactive recovery works to
shorten MTTR from 30s to 15-20s but cannot avoid the basic window of downtime.
Aggressive health checking (1s intervals) causes computational overhead and false
positives in the case of legitimate load spikes (Yang & Kim, 2020). Over-provisioning
results in a wasting of 40-60% infrastructure resources with no protection for correlated
failures.

A different approach is available in proactive fault tolerance with failure prediction
and preventive migration. Research has shown that proactive techniques can minimize
failures and reduce the use of resources and energy significantly compared to reactive
techniques (Tian et al., 2020). However, the implementation requires accurate
prediction without false positives, light weight monitoring with minimal overhead and
zero-downtime migration mechanisms. SwarmGuard solves these issues with rulebased thresholds and Docker Swarm's built-in rolling update API.

1.3 Project Objectives
This research project is focused on the design, implementation and validation of a
rule-based proactive recovery mechanism for containerized applications running on
Docker Swarm with zero downtime recovery using predictive monitoring and contextaware recovery strategies. The proposed system, SwarmGuard, solves the basic
limitations of the reactive recovery approaches by identifying early warning signs of
container failure before the full service degradation, applying intelligent recovery
strategies according to the failure context, and performing preventive actions without
service interruption. Unlike machine learning based prediction systems, which require
significant amounts of training data and computational costs, SwarmGuard has
lightweight rule-based thresholds that are optimized for resource limited small and
medium enterprise (SME) environments that run on legacy network infrastructure. The
system integrates seamlessly with Docker Swarm's native API and orchestrator
mechanisms, eliminating the need for platform migration while providing enterprise
grade improve reliability.

3

Objectives:
1.To design and develop a lightweight, real-time monitoring and decision system that
detects early warning signs of container failure through continuous tracking of
resource utilization metrics (CPU, memory, network I/O) and applies context-aware
recovery strategies to distinguish between node-specific problems requiring migration
and cluster-wide traffic surges requiring horizontal scaling.
2.To design and develop zero-downtime container migration and intelligent autoscaling mechanisms that integrate with Docker Swarm's native API to execute
recovery actions (container migration between nodes, replica scaling) without service
interruption, using rolling updates with constraint-based placement manipulation and
graceful connection draining.
3.To validate the effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed proactive recovery
mechanism through comprehensive performance evaluation on physical hardware
infrastructure, measuring key metrics including Mean Time To Recovery (MTTR),
system overhead, recovery success rate, and comparative performance against Docker
Swarm's native reactive recovery baseline.

1.4 Scope of Project
This research is Docker Swarm specific, and fills a gap in the literature: while
Kubernetes is the main focus of academic research, Docker Swarm continues to be
adopted by SMEs. The experimental testbed is a five-node servers connected by
100Mbps switches, intentionally to mimic resource-constrained SME environments.
The cluster consists of 1 manager node and 4 worker nodes with some additional
devices for observability (InfluxDB, Grafana) and distributed load generation.

SwarmGuard tracks three metrics on a container level, CPU percentage, memory
percentage, and network throughput (Mbps). Metrics collection is performed using the
Stats API in Docker with 5 second polling intervals, to provide a balance between
detection latency and overhead of the monitoring system. The system is not focused on
infrastructure-level monitoring, but instead on containerized workload health.
4

The system has two proactive strategies. Scenario 1 (node specific problems) triggers
container migration using Docker Swarm in rolling update with start-first ordering and
constraint based placement. Scenario 2 (traffic conditions are high) triggers horizontal
auto-scaling by modifying service replicas The system does NOT implement vertical
scaling, affinity-based rescheduling or application-level recovery patterns.

SwarmGuard uses rule-based thresholds versus machine learning. The decision
engine is used for classifying the scenarios based on boolean logic: resource violations
(CPU > 70% OR memory > 70%) AND network state (>65Mbps = high network traffic,
<35Mbps = node problems = high). This design removes training data requirements and
delivers transparent and immediately deployable recovery logic.

Validation is 30 controlled iterations: 10 baseline tests, 10 Scenario 1 tests, 10
Scenario 2 tests. All experiments use a custom FastAPI stress application with
controllable CPU, memory and network stress endpoints. Performance measures
include MTTR (number of seconds), monitoring overhead (CPU%, memory MB,
network Mbps), scaling latency, quality of load distribution.

SwarmGuard is NOT a replacement of Kubernetes or a production ready enterprise
solution with HA guarantees. The recovery manager is a single point of failure. The
system does NOT support a stateful application, multi-cloud deployment, or cluster
federation. Security implications, advanced networking features (service mesh,
encrypted overlays) and all-round fault injection diversity are out of scope.

1.5 Significance of Project
Container failures due to CPU/Memory exhaustion and network failures greatly
affect small and medium scale Docker Swarm deployments that do not have
sophisticated recovery infrastructure. Default reactive recovery mechanisms lead to

5

guaranteed downtime in the event of failures and has an impact on service availability
and user experience.

SwarmGuard presents a lightweight, rule-based proactive recovery framework which
can detect early warning signs and prevent full-scale outages. The proactive approach
enhances system availability and lowers MTTR and eliminates unnecessary container
restarts from reactive recovery. Unlike ML-based systems that have high computational
needs and do not offer interpretable and configurable decision-making, the rule-based
design offers interpretable and deployable logic that does not need data science
expertise.

DevOps teams managing Docker Swarm achieve cost-effective resilience
improvements with no more hardware or cloud dependencies. Researchers are able to
benefit from documented light weight recovery strategies that apply to edge and onpremises systems. End users have better service reliability, less downtime and
continuity of operation.

This project serves to advance the container orchestration and designing of faulttolerant system with demonstration of proactive and rule-based recovery optimized for
Docker Swarm. It validates the existence of lightweight, transparent reliability
mechanisms to achieve high availability in distributed container environments without
ML complexity.

LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
Container orchestration platforms such as Docker Swarm and Kubernetes have
changed how applications are deployed by automating the container lifecycle
management (Casalicchio & Iannucci, 2020). However, reactive fault recovery
mechanisms cause guaranteed service downtime in the event of container failures,
which is at odds with modern high availability requirements. This literature review
covers the evolution of container orchestration, issues in reactive recovery, proactive
approaches and research gaps that SwarmGuard fills.

Figure 1: Evolution of Application Deployment Technologies
(Source: Adapted from Bentaleb et al., 2022; Tesser & Borin, 2023)

Figure 1 shows the development of application deployment technologies in three
different eras. The Bare Metal Era (1990s - 2000s) saw direct hardware deployment
with recovery times measured in hours. The era of Virtualization (2000s-2010s) brought
the VM-based isolation with a recovery time in minutes. The modern Container Era

7

(2013-Present) has sub-second recovery times with much improved deployment
efficiency.

Different container orchestration platforms are available aside from Kubernetes and
Docker Swarm, with different approaches to the architecture and trade-offs (Moravcik
& Kontsek, 2021).

2.2 Container Orchestration Platforms
2.2.1 Docker Swarm Architecture
Docker Swarm is a native container orchestration built directly into Docker Engine,
which is a simpler alternative to Kubernetes if the organization needs to implement
orchestration without operation complexity (Docker Inc., 2024). The architecture is
manager-worker topology with manager nodes having cluster state kept through Raft
consensus protocol and worker nodes running containerized workloads.

Key features relevant to fault recovery are service abstraction, which offers
declarative service definitions with desired numbers of replicas, health checks, which
offers container level health monitoring via either a health check on an http or tcp
connection or a health check command with configurable time interval and thresholds
and automatic rescheduling, which allows manager nodes to detect the failed
containers and reschedule the replacements on healthy workers. The ingress routing
mesh distributes traffic from outside into the service using the round-robin loadbalancing mechanism and the embedded DNS server is used to resolve the service
names to container IP addresses.

Event-driven architecture has become a powerful architecture for developing
scalable, resilient distributed systems. Sharma et al (2025) document the use of eventdriven architectures, in which services are able to scale independently using
decoupled event flows, with message brokers buffering events to smooth demand
spikes, a good match to Docker Swarm's service-oriented deployment model.
8

Figure 2: Docker Swarm Architecture
(Source: Adapted from Docker Documentation, 2024)

Figure 2 shows the Docker Swarm architecture with its two tier node structure.
Manager nodes, comprised of Leaders and Followers, ensure cluster state consistency
using a consensus protocol called Raft. Worker nodes receive and run containerized
tasks that are sent via the control plane to ensure a distributed workload execution.

Manager node responsibilities include acceptance of API requests, orchestration of
cluster state, scheduling of containers to worker nodes based on resource availability
and constraints, and maintenance of service definitions and desired state. Worker
nodes will execute the containers, report the health status and communicate with the
manager nodes via secure channels using the TLS protocol. The Raft consensus
algorithm has the property of consistency among the manager nodes, it requires
quorum (N/2 + 1) for operations in the cluster, and it supports automatic leader
election in the event of manager failures.
9

2.2.2 Kubernetes Architecture (Comparative Context)
Kubernetes is the king of the container orchestration world with 83% market share
by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation's 2023 annual survey (CNCF, 2024). Its
architecture offers more comprehensive features with more complexity of operation
by having multiple components such as etcd for state storage, kube-apiserver for API
operations, kube-scheduler for pod placement, and kubelet agents in worker nodes.

Kubernetes provides sophisticated ways to schedule, through custom schedulers
and priority classes, support for namespace-based multi-tenancy using resource
quotas, a wide range of networking mechanisms using Container Network Interface
(CNI) plugins, and sophisticated workload types such as StatefulSets, DaemonSets,
and Jobs. However, this flexibility comes with a much higher degree of operational
know-how compared to the integrated approach of Docker Swarm.

2.2.3 Container Scheduling Algorithms
Container scheduling algorithms are responsible for determining the best
placement of workloads across nodes in a cluster with the goal of balancing utilization
of resources, fault tolerance, and any application-specific constraints. Docker Swarm
involves a two-phase scheduling process that involves filtering and scoring phases
(Docker Inc., 2024).

10

Figure 3: Container Scheduling Algorithm
(Source: Adapted from Carrión, 2022; Ahmad et al., 2022)

Figure 3 shows the two phase container scheduling algorithm that Docker Swarm
uses. Filtering phase removes the ineligible nodes based on resource availability and
constraint requirements. The ensuing scoring phase ranks remaining candidates based
on balance of resources, locality of images and configured affinity rules to optimize
placement decisions.

In the filtering phase, nodes that fail to meet container requirements are filtered
out, taking into account available CPU and memory resources, node constraints
specified via labels, taint toleration rules and image availability. Nodes that do not
meet with minimum requirements are not considered for scheduling.

The scoring phase scores eligible nodes based on weighted criteria including
resource balance preferring nodes which have balanced CPU and memory utilization,
11

in order to avoid resource fragmentation, image locality preferring nodes which have
the required container images already cached, reducing the latency at container startup
time, spread preferences distributing replicas across different nodes for fault tolerance
and affinity rules respecting user-defined co-location or anti-co-location
requirements. The scheduler chooses the best scoring node to place the containers
with round-robin distribution as secondary.

2.3 Challenges in Reactive Fault Recovery
2.3.1 Detection Latency and Guaranteed Downtime
Reactive recovery systems rely on health check mechanisms to detect the failed
containers. Docker Swarm provides the ability to implement health probes that are
implemented via the use of the following: HTTP, TCP, Command-based health probes
which can be configured with customizable intervals (the default value is 30 seconds)
and customizable failure thresholds (the default value is 3 consecutive failures). This
design comes with a built-in detection latency of 10-90 seconds based on the health
check frequency and failure threshold configuration.

Figure 4 illustrates the reactive recovery timeline of the previous Docker Swarm
deployments. Container failure detection takes 20-40 seconds using health check
mechanisms and is followed by the container restart process. This leads to total service
unavailability in the range of 35-55 seconds even under the best conditions, the
drawbacks of reactive approaches are apparent.

2.3.2 Resource Exhaustion Detection Gaps
Resource exhaustion is the most common form of failure in containerized systems,
and similar with gradual detection mechanisms are slow to detect the gradual
degradation, before it should lead to a catastrophic failure (Tran et al., 2022).
Container health checks are generally used to check the responsiveness of applications
by means of an http endpoint or process existence checks, but cannot detect
application performance slowdown due to memory pressure, throttling of cpus or
saturation of disk I/O.

13

Figure 6: Resource Exhaustion Detection Gap

Figure 6 shows the gap in the detection of critical resource exhaustion in reactive

A container with a memory leak can have normal responses to health checks for
minutes or even hours with memory consumption gradually rising to the Out-ofMemory (OOM) kill threshold. Similarly, requests can suffer from noisy neighbor
containers causing CPU resource contention that produces a slowdown for request
processing but passes through binary health checks. By the time health checks fail,
the quality of the service has already deteriorated quite a bit and users have
experienced degraded service performance.

2.3.3 State Management During Failures
Reactive recovery mechanisms are very difficult for stateful applications as
container restarts lose in-memory state and open connections. This limitation is
especially relevant for applications that handle user sessions, manage file uploads,
maintain WebSocket connections or manage distributed transactions.
14

For web applications that are processing file uploads it is common to have a
container failure in the middle of the transfer, meaning clients must restart uploads
from scratch, wasting bandwidth and negatively impacting user experience. Similarly,
WebSocket connections for real-time functionalities like chat application or live
notification end suddenly during reactive recovery with the need for client-side
reconnect logic with a possible loss of events in the disconnection window (Singh &
Singh, 2022).

Managing stateful applications is a special challenge for container orchestration
platforms. Vayghan et al. (2021) explored availability management for stateful
applications that are based on microservices and it was presented to the case study
that the default platform repair actions could not be sufficient for the availability of
the applications and they suggested an enhanced State Controller that improved the
recovery times by 55-99% based on automatic service redirection.

Database connection pools make reactive recovery even more complex. When a
container is restarted, existing database connections become invalid, leading to
transaction failures for in-flight requests. Applications need to put in place
sophisticated retry logic, connection pool recovery logic, and idempotency guarantees
to handle the sudden connection loss gracefully. Legacy applications that were not
built for container environments do not necessarily have these kinds of resilience
features so reactive recovery can prove especially disruptive.

Session management strategies have significant effect on reactive recovery
behavior. Applications that use in-memory session storage lose all user sessions with
container restarts, requiring users to re-authenticate, or lose shopping carts, form data,
and/or the progress of a workflow. While this issue is mitigated by external session
stores (Redis, Memcached) they add other dependencies to infrastructure and
additional latency overhead. Sticky session configurations in load balancers cause a

15

lack of flexibility in container placement, and can cause uneven load distribution
across replicas.

2.3.4 Impact on Service Level Agreements
Service Level Agreements (SLAs) are used to define uptime contractual
arrangements with 99.9% uptime allowing for 43.8 minutes of annual downtime and
99.99% uptime allowing for only 52.6 minutes of downtime for all causes including
planned maintenance and unexpected failures. Reactive recovery systems with Mean
Time To Recovery MTTR from 60 to 360 seconds waste significant portions of this
downtime budget with each failure event. For example, three container failures per
month, each taking 180 seconds to recover, will cause 9 minutes of monthly downtime
- which is too much for the monthly budget (approx. 4.38 minutes allowed for a
99.99% SLA) and could lead to financial penalties or service credits based on
customer contracts.

SLA measurement methodologies add to the problems of reactive recovery. Some
providers report availability as the percentage of successful responses to health
checks, while some providers monitor actual rates of user-facing request success.
Reactive recovery systems might provide a high availability from an infrastructure
perspective (nodes up, containers running) with a user experience of service
disruption during the 60-360 second recovery window. This difference between
infrastructure factors and user experience reveals the inadequacy of reactive
approaches for applications that demand genuine high availability, especially of
financial applications and critical infrastructure systems that demand 99.999% (five
nines) availability, with only 5.26 minutes of annual downtime.

2.4 Proactive Recovery Approaches
Fault tolerance is very crucial for high availability and reliability in cloud computing
environments. Rehman et al. (2022) provide a comprehensive survey that deals with
fault tolerance mechanisms. Approaches are categorized in two types: reactive
approaches that recover systems after faults occur, and proactive approaches that predict
16

and prevent failures before they occur using techniques like preemptive migration and
self-healing mechanisms.

2.4.1 Machine Learning vs Rule-Based Approaches
Academic research covers two types of proactive recovery methods: predictive
failure methods using ML and the rule-based threshold systems. Dang-Quang and Yoo
(2021) showed Bi-LSTM prediction models have 530-600 times faster prediction
speed than ARIMA models with better resource provision accuracy and elastic
speedup than standard LSTM approaches. Their further work (Dang-Quang & Yoo,
2022) demonstrated that multivariate Bi-LSTM had RMSE prediction error nearly
1.84x smaller than univariate approaches to make more accurate proactive autoscaling
decisions.

Rule-based systems use pre-defined threshold values on observable metrics to
initiate proactive recovery actions. When the CPU usage of containers goes above
75% for 30 consecutive seconds or memory consumption crosses 90% threshold
values, the system triggers preventive migration before health check failures happen.
These systems provide predictable behavior, low computation overhead (typically
below 2% CPU), and easy troubleshooting but have a hard time handling dynamic
patterns of workload as well as manually tuning the thresholds for different
application profiles.

Clustering techniques with proactive scheduling can enhance fault tolerance in
scientific workflows in the cloud. Farhood et al. (2025) proposed a scheduling
approach that combines proactive techniques with task clustering, which showed the
improvement in the makespan, cost, energy consumption, and failure rate in a
company as compared to reactive methods.

17

2.4.2 Threshold-Based Detection Systems
Threshold-based proactive systems set tangible resource utilization limits that will
lead to recovery actions before failures occur. It's a common industry practice to use
resource utilization thresholds in the 80-percent range when it comes to triggering
autoscaling policies (Park & Jeong, 2023). Park and Jeong (2023) showed that the
best performance can be achieved with 99% improvement of elastic speedup over
purely reactive approaches by combining 80% network throughput threshold and
proactive deep learning-based prediction at 0.43ms of additional computational
overhead. Memory thresholds are usually on the range between 85-95% depending
on the application characteristics as lower thresholds are suited for applications
suffering from memory leaks while higher thresholds are for applications with
predictable memory consumption patterns.

Figure 7: Threshold-Based Detection Challenges

Figure 7 shows the dual challenges associated with static threshold-based detection
systems. A fixed 75% CPU threshold is causing 5 false positive alerts during normal
traffic spikes which leads to unnecessary container restarts of healthy services. On the
other hand, the same threshold cannot detect a gradual performance degradation
which is below 75%, leading to false negatives where the container may enter
catastrophic failure states undetected.

Some basic problems with static thresholds. Traffic spikes result in very short CPU
spikes over thresholds but resolve themselves naturally in seconds whereas memory
leaks are slower growing patterns that can remain below thresholds until sudden Out18

of-Memory termination occurs. Multi-condition thresholds ( combining CPU and
memory under a duration), however, reduce false positives and increase the detection
latency and implementation complexity.

Dynamic threshold adjustment adjusts according to application behavior patterns
learned from past metrics. Toka et al. (2020) Adaptive Auto-scaling for Kubernetes:
AI-based automatic resource adjustment using workload patterns Auto-scaling for
applications with workload patterns that change over time is achieved by using autoscaling. However, this approach requires a certain amount of historical data and may
not work well with unprecedented loads such as viral content surges and/or DDoS
attacks which exceed learned patterns.

Time window considerations have a major effect on the threshold effectiveness.
Short (10-15 seconds) window time is used for fast detection but leads to sensitivity
to transient spikes, and long (60-120 seconds) window time is used for stability but
slows down preventive action. Industry implementations usually use 30-second
windows as a tradeoff between responsiveness and stability, but for a particular
application type and infrastructure capacity, these values may be better suited.

2.5 Container Migration and Zero-Downtime Strategies
2.5.1 Migration Techniques
Checkpoint/Restore In Userspace (CRIU) is a library that allows for true live
migration by serializing full process state including memory pages, open file
descriptors, network connections and pending signals. Guitart (2024) achieve 800ms
migration times for stateful containerized applications using optimized CRIU precopy memory transfer, where frequently modified memory pages are transferred
iteratively before final state snapshot. This approach allows active TCP connections,
in-progress file I/O operations and application memory state to be preserved without
the interruption of service.

However, CRIU has many strict requirement for its operation: Linux 3.11+ with
checkpoint/restore system calls, root privileges to manipulate memory and control
processes, application compatibility without incompatible operations such as external
shared memory or some security modules. CRIU has serious limitations in
compatibility for production environment. Andrijauskas et al. (2024) has found that
CRIU cannot checkpoint containerized workloads when invoked externally, does not
support GPU at all and requires identical versions of the libraries between source and
destination systems. These limitations implicitly prohibit CRIU adoption for
applications that have GPU dependencies, use nested container architectures, or
require cross-platform migration, requiring alternative methods such as checkpointing
at the application level.

21

Figure 8: Container Migration Strategies
(Source: Adapted from Tran et al., 2022)

Figure 8 is a comparison of the stateless and stateful approaches of container
migration. Stateless migration initiates a new container instance and adjusts the
routing rules and clears the existing connections from the old container and completes
the process in 5-10 seconds. Stateful migration with CRIU is required to checkpoint
state of a process, transfer process checkpoint images to the target node, sync storage
volumes and restore container, which requires 5-130 seconds depending on storage
size and the network bandwidth.

Docker swarm has a rolling update mechanism that provides operational simplicity
sacrificing state preservation. The --update-order start-first setting will launch
replacement containers before it kills originals, again allowing for a short overlap in
which both versions can serve requests. The ingress routing mesh gradually redirects
traffic to new containers and kills off old connections over configurable timeouts
(default 10 seconds). This stateless approach makes external session storage or session
loss on updates a requirement for applications.

22

CRIU provides 800ms downtime with full state preservation but requires kernel
support and root access whereas rolling updates provide 5-10 seconds connection
draining with partial availability but do not require special privileges and can be used
with any containerized application. For the rolling update environment that
SwarmGuard is deployed in, it is the best tradeoff between simplicity of operations
and acceptable overhead of migration for stateless microservices.

Connection draining enables graceful migration without letting terminating
containers hold additional requests, and allows in-flight requests to finish. Docker
Swarm does its implementation using the ingress routing mesh, which identifies
"draining" containers by listening for the SIGTERM signal and ceases to route new

Session persistence strategies: When an application is migrating, session
persistence strategies define the application behavior. Sticky sessions set up in load
balancers attach user sessions to certain container instances to make application
development easier, but migration is complicated as all sessions end when a container
goes down. Stateless session management with JWT tokens or external session stores
(Redis, Memcached) allows for transparent migration as new containers have access
to the same session information, at the cost of dependencies on infrastructure and
potential consistency issues.

There is more complexity with the keep-alive connections using the http protocol.
Long-lived connections for server-sent events or streaming responses may outlive
stop grace periods, so application-level work is needed to ensure that connections can
be gracefully closed when termination signals are received. Modern frameworks have
lifecycle hooks in which applications can broadcast shutdown notifications to the
clients which can be utilized to connect gracefully to other container instances.

Best practices for graceful shutdown are including registering handler for
SIGTNERM as a means to initiate shutdown sequence, not accepting new requests
but continuing processing active requests, closing idle keep-alive connections and
retaining active request connections, flushing pending writes to databases or message
queues, and sending final health check responses, which indicate unavailability.
Implementation of these practices minimizes user-visible disruption for proactive
migration, but applications that have not been designed for container environments
may not have necessary shutdown logic.

2.6 Monitoring Infrastructure
Modern observability platforms such as Datadog and New Relic provide full-stack
monitoring capabilities for containerized environments, albeit with very different
feature sets and pricing (Better Stack, 2025).

2.6.1 Push vs Pull Monitoring Models
Container monitoring architectures have two main data collection models. Pullbased systems (represented by Prometheus) periodically pull measurements from their
metrics endpoints at configured intervals (typically 15-60 seconds), while push-based
systems (InfluxDB with InfluxDB agents) have monitoring agents actively pushing
measurements to central collection infrastructure at typical sub-second intervals. The
architectural decision has a fundamental effect on the proactive recovery capabilities,
in pull-based monitoring there is inherent latency between metrics generation and
central visibility, while push-based systems offer near real-time monitoring of metrics.

Figure 9 shows architectural differences between push and pull models. Pull-based
monitoring assigns responsibility to the central server to discover the targets and begin
collecting the metrics, which simplifies agent deployment, but necessitates service
discovery mechanisms and network accessibility from the monitoring server to all the
targets. Push-based monitoring reverses this relationship, with agents establishing
connections to central infrastructure, supporting NAT traversal and firewall-restricted
environments while demanding agent installation and configuration to each monitored
node.

Push and pull monitoring architectures are compared in figure 9. The push model
(left) uses agents that actively send metrics using an HTTP post to a central database
25

that can deliver metrics in real time with sub-second latencies. The pull model (right)
has a central server that periodically retrieves metrics endpoints using the robust
protocol (HTTP GET), which eliminates the complexity of agent deployment at the
expense of longer polling times.

Figure 9: Push vs Pull Monitoring Architecture Comparison
(Source: Adapted from InfluxData, 2024)

For proactive recovery systems where the need is to detect the exhaustion of
resources quickly, push-based monitoring has huge advantages. InfluxDB agents are
able to provide metrics at one-second intervals or more, which can identify a sudden
spike in CPU or memory growth within seconds of their occurrence. Pull-based
Prometheus systems running with 15-30 seconds scrape intervals may not be able to
detect transient resource spikes that happen between scrape intervals, limiting the
accuracy of detecting short-lived failure conditions.

26

2.6.2 Time-Series Databases for Metrics Storage
Time-series databases are optimized for metric storage patterns with sequential
writes with a timestamp, time range queries for visualization and analysis, and high
ingestion rates from distributed sources. InfluxDB uses a Time-Structured Merge
(TSM) tree engine with 250,000+ writes/second on commodity hardware using
batching and compression (InfluxData, 2024). The schema-less tag-based data model
supports flexible data metric dimensions with no predefined schemas to support
dynamic container environments where services and instances come and go
frequently.

Storage architecture in time series databases is balanced between the
considerations of write throughput, query performance and data retention. InfluxDB
creates data shards that represent time shards (ex. 1 hour or 1 day) so that older shards
are compacted to save space and the data is kept write optimized for recent data.
Retention policies automatically remove expired data according to age, avoiding
unlimited storage size increases while retaining recent history for operational queries
and long-term trends for capacity planning.

Query optimization techniques that are specific to time series data include:
continuous queries for pre-aggregating common calculations (hourly averages, daily
maximums), down sampling to decrease the data resolution for older time periods
while keeping recent high-resolution data, and indexing on tag dimensions to speed
filtering on the container ID, service name, or node hostname. These optimizations
allow for responsive queries in the dashboard across millions of metric points without
being a burden on the database resources.

2.6.3 Real-Time Visualization with Grafana
Grafana offers real-time visualization of time series metrics via dashboards of
graphs, gauges, tables and alert indicators. For container monitoring, common
dashboards have a list of CPU and memory utilization of containers over time,
network I/O bandwidth usage metrics, health check success rate and failure count
27

metrics, container restart events timeline, and cluster-wide resource allocation
metrics. Template variables as this allows single dashboard designs to display metrics
for multiple services, nodes or environments, using dropdowns.

Alert configuration in Grafana identifies situations when teams that handle
operations should be alerted. For proactive monitoring, alert rules check if the CPU
usage of containers is above 80% for 30 consecutive seconds, if the memory
consumption crosses the threshold of 90%, if the health check failure rate is above
threshold of 10% in 5 minutes window, or if the count of container restart exceeds
expected container restart count. Notification channels are used to provide email,
Slack, PagerDuty or webhook integrations that allow automated response systems to
trigger recovery actions.

Query optimization for real-time dashboards involves finding the right balance of
data freshness and system load. Auto-Refresh interval of 5-10 seconds would give
near-real time visibility without overloading time-series databases and dashboard time
ranges should be limited to recent hours or days to limit the scope of queries. Proper
use of aggregation functions (moving averages, rate calculations), Reduces the
number of data points displayed without losing the visibility of trend, making the
dashboard more responsive and easier to interpret.

2.7 Auto-Scaling Mechanisms
2.7.1 Horizontal vs Vertical Scaling
Container auto-scaling solves capacity issues in either horizontal (adding replica
containers) or vertical (increasing the resource limits for existing containers) ways.
Horizontal scaling, involves distribution of load to multiple and identical container
instances behind a load balancer, Linear Scalability, Fault Tolerance (load balancer
provides redundancy), Zero Downtime Scaling Operations. Vertical Scaling In
Vertical scaling exists the option to increase the CPU and memory limit for already
running containers, which can be a simple way to scale up single instance
28

applications, but will necessitate both container restart, and limited headroom for
scaling operations constrained by the node resources.

(Source: Adapted from Kubernetes Documentation, 2024)

A comparison of horizontal and vertical scaling strategies for containerized
applications is shown in Figure 10. Horizontal Scaling (Left), replica instances are

2.7.2 Cooldown Periods and Oscillation Prevention
Auto-scaling systems must avoid oscillation: A quick scale-up followed by scaledown in response to transient load spikes is wasteful and destabilizes services (Dogani
et al., 2023). Cooldown periods enforce minimum intervals between scaling actions;
29

this could normally be 60-300 seconds between actions, based on application startup
time and the volatility of the load pattern. During cooldown, the system will monitor
the metrics, but it will not make immediate scaling decisions even if thresholds are
exceeded, to give newly created containers time to initialize and share the load before
deciding if additional scaling is needed.

Hysteresis in scaling thresholds makes there are different trigger points for scaleup versus scale-down actions. For example, CPU utilization more than 80% may
trigger scale-up and scale-down requires CPU to be lower than 40% for 5 minutes.
This asymmetry keeps us away from fast oscillations on either side of the threshold
boundaries where short metric fluctuations will make continuous scaling actions. The
difference between scale-up and scale-down thresholds (in the example 40%
difference) should be larger than normal workload variation to be sure that the
workload is operated stably.

2.8 Network-Aware Failure Classification
2.8.1 Context-Aware Recovery Decisions
A critical issue in proactive recovery is that it can be hard to tell apart containerspecific issues that need to be migrated and legitimate traffic surges that need to be
scaled. Both situations involve high CPU utilization; however, responses are
fundamentally different, migration means moving containers under stress to
underutilized nodes, while scaling refers to augmenting capacity to meet higher
demand. Incorrect classification results in unnecessary migrations during traffic peaks
(wasting resources and potentially destabilizing clusters) or unsuccessful scaling
attempts in the case of underlying problems being container-specific resource
exhaustion.

Figure 11 shows SwarmGuard's network-aware classification approach, where
network traffic metrics are used as a discriminating factor. High CPU (greater than
80%) and high network traffic would be indicative of legitimate processing of requests
30

(Scenario 2: Traffic Load requiring scaling), whereas high CPU and low network
activity would indicate computational stress (i.e., high CPU usage and low network
traffic) associated with inefficient algorithms, background processing or resource
contention (Scenario 1: Container Stress requiring migration).

The network-aware mechanism of classifying the scenarios of failures. The system
correlates between CPU utilization and network I/O metrics in order to distinguish
between traffic load situations (high CPU and high network activity) and resource
exhaustion situations (high CPU and low network activity) in order to make contextaware recovery decisions.

Figure 11: Network-Aware Scenario Classification

The context-aware decision flow shown in Figure 11 is used to map classified
scenarios to best recovery actions with sub-second latency. When Scenario 1
(container stress) is detected, constraint-based migration is triggered by SwarmGuard
using Docker Swarm's rolling update mechanism. When Scenario 2 (traffic surge) is
detected, SwarmGuard makes a horizontal scaling by incrementing service replica
count. This differentiation helps to avoid common pitfalls where generic autoscaling

2.8.2 Multi-Metric Correlation Analysis
Multi-metric correlation identifies causal relationships between resource metrics
that are not indicated by single-metric monitoring. High CPU usage coupled with high
network throughput generally means processing legitimate traffic, where application
servers are using the CPU cycles processing an incoming volume of requests from the
user-space (via an appropriate web server), queries to the database server, or API calls.
32

On the other hand, high CPU with low network activity indicates topics of
computation stress due to batch processing that runs in the background, inefficient
algorithm processing, or CPU-intensive work that does not pertain to user requests
(Wang et al., 2025).

Similarly, memory growth was correlated with request count patterns pointing to
normal caching behavior or session accumulation while uncorrelated memory growth
may be a symptom of memory leaks that require container restart. Disk I/O patterns
that are correlated with volume of database queries provide a good indication of
expected behavior, but elevated disk I/O without corresponding activity from the
applications is a clue to the log file growth, temporary file accumulation, or systemlevel disk contention from other processes.

Correlation analysis techniques use statistical measures such as Pearson correlation
coefficients computed over sliding periods of time (usually 5-15 minutes) as a way of
quantifying the relationships between metrics. Correlation values above 0.7 would
indicate positive relationships of strong strength suggesting causal connection, and
values below 0.3 would suggest independent variation to be interpreted differently.
Time-lag analysis takes into account delayed effects, such as memory allocation
seconds following request peaks, or CPU throttling several minutes after thermal
events.

Implementation challenges include the determination of proper timeframes for
correlation calculation (trading responsiveness with statistical significance), missing
data in the case of failure to collect metrics intermittently, and interpretation of
correlation in the case of fast-changing workload conditions where relationships
change over the course of minutes. Production systems usually combine the
correlation analysis with domain-specific rules of the expected behavior of the
application instead of relying only on statistical measures.

2.9.1 SwarmGuard's Differentiation from Existing Research
The comparative analysis in Table 2 shows some significant gaps that SwarmGuard
covers. Academic research focuses mostly on Kubernetes environments (Tran et al.,
2022; Carreno et al., 2022; Dang-Quang and Yoo, 2021; Vayghan et al., 2021; Toka
38

et al., 2020) leaving aside the Docker Swarm ecosystem serving millions of SME
deployments. SwarmGuard addresses this underserved platform specifically and
offers proactive recovery capabilities without having to incur the operational
complexity of Kubernetes.

Due to the recent research, machine learning approaches to failure prediction are
becoming more common. Dang-Quang and Yoo (2021) use bidirectional LSTM
networks that are 530-600 times faster for predicting than ARIMA models, while
keeping the accuracy of resource provision superior, and Toka et al. (2020) use
reinforcement learning for adaptive scaling. However, these ML-based systems
require a lot of training data (6 + months), computational resources (10-15% CPU
overhead), and cannot be interpreted. SwarmGuard has made a deliberate decision to
use threshold-based detection (at the expense of ML's ability to recognize patterns)
because it offers immediate deployment, transparent decision-making and low
resource overhead (less than 5% CPU).

Live container migration research is on CRIU techniques. Soussi et al. (2024) note
that, in order to achieve true stateful migration with less than 1 second downtime,
complex integration in the kernel is necessary. Guitart (2024) show 500ms migration
times while Di et al. (2021) show 30% less migration time compared to sequential
migration with their use of a multicontainer migration strategy. SwarmGuard is
avoiding these complications by using the native rolling update mechanism of Docker
Swarm with competitive migration (0.6 seconds average) without kernel changes.

SwarmGuard makes a contribution by its context-aware classification, which
differentiates between container-specific issues that need to be scaled out and
legitimate traffic surges that need to be scaled up. By adding network traffic
information to CPU and memory monitoring, SwarmGuard's decision engine
computes whether resource consumption is due to application load (leading to
horizontal scaling action), or the deterioration of a container (leading to migration

39

action) to avoid inappropriate recovery actions typical for generic auto-scaling
frameworks (Dogani et al., 2023).

2.10 Summary and Research Gap Identification
This literature review covered the work that has been done for container
orchestration, fault tolerance mechanisms, proactive recovery approaches, migration
techniques, monitoring architectures and autoscaling systems. Container orchestration
platforms have been widely adopted with Kubernetes leading the market (83% market
share) but Docker Swarm still holds significant market share (10% adoption) among
SMEs (who prefer operational simplicity).

There are four critical gaps that SwarmGuard addresses in a literature review. First,
Docker Swarm Proactive Recovery Gap: a lot of research in Kubernetes but lack of
Docker Swarm work despite huge SME adoption. Second, Context-Aware Decision
Gap: existing systems have generic responses to failures without distinguishing the
types of failures. Third, Zero-Downtime Migration Gap: Due to the academic focus on
complex CRIU approaches, the practical focus on Docker Swarm constraint-based
scheduling remains under-explored. Fourth, SME-Focused Solutions Gap: research
focuses on large scale resource-rich environments and resource-constrained situations
are understudied.

SwarmGuard solves such gaps with proactive monitoring specifically for Docker
Swarm, context-aware classification based on network metrics, zero-downtime
migration based on rolling update with constraint-based placement and networkoptimized design verified on resource constrained infrastructure (less than 0.5Mbps
overhead, less than 5% CPU).

3.2.1 Overall System Architecture
SwarmGuard's architecture consists of 4 integrated subsystems: Monitoring Layer
for collecting container metrics, Decision Layer for threshold evaluation and failure
classification, Execution Layer for recovery actions through Docker Swarm API, and
the Knowledge Layer for historical data and system state. Complete system
architecture with component interactions and data flows is shown in figure 13.

Figure 13: SwarmGuard System Architecture

Figure 13 shows the full architecture of the SwarmGuard system divided into 4
integrated layers. The monitoring layer consists of Python agents and InfluxDB to
collect and store the metrics. The decision layer consists of a python Flask recovery
manager for smart failure classification. The execution layer in turn interfaces with
the Docker Swarm API for automatic recovery actions. The observability layer gives
Grafana dashboards for real-time visualization. This event-driven architecture allows
the monitoring agents to detect the violation of thresholds and to send the alerts to the
recovery manager through the http protocol, which then performs the right recovery
actions using the Docker API.

detection. Network traffic correlation differentiates between container stress (high
CPU, low network) and traffic surge (high CPU, high network), making it possible to
classify events on context with average alert generation time of 7-9 milliseconds.

3.2.2 Proactive Recovery Workflow
SwarmGuard's proactive recovery has five sequential phases as shown in Figure
14. Monitoring Phase is used for gathering real-time metrics using distributed Python
agents on each worker node. Analysis Phase examines local threshold conditions of
the monitoring agents, finding stable violations (2+ consecutive intervals).
Classification Phase involves network-aware decision logic separating container
stress (network < 35Mbps) that requires migration from traffic surge (network >
65Mbps) that requires scaling.

using Python Docker SDK and saves results in the InfluxDB to perform analysis.
Average end-to-end latency from metric collection to recovery initiation is 7-15
seconds based on threshold breach duration requirements.

3.2.3 Network-Aware Failure Classification
A key innovation in SwarmGuard is network traffic correlation to differentiate the
type of failure. Traditional threshold based systems use single metric or dual metric
evaluation (CPU alone) or dual metric evaluation (CPU and memory) without
differentiating the failure causes. High CPU usage can be indicative of containerspecific issues (resource leaks, inefficient algorithms) that need to be addressed via
migration, or legitimate increases in traffic that need to be handled via scaling.

SwarmGuard brings the network I/O as discriminating factor. High CPU (>80%)
along with high network traffic (>65 Mbps) - This is an indication of legitimate load
which requires capacity expansion through more replicas (Scenario 2). High CPU
with low network traffic (<10 Mbps) indicates container specific problems that need
to be migrated to healthy infrastructure (Scenario 1). Intermediate network traffic (3565% of capacity or 35-65 Mbps on 100Mbps networks) creates a dead zone in
classification where high resource usage does NOT cause automatic recovery. The
monitoring agent does not raise alerts for this range, because the system cannot be
sure whether the problem is a migration or scaling problem.

3.2.4 Zero-Downtime Migration Strategy
SwarmGuard performs zero downtime migration using Docker Swarm's native
rolling update mechanism instead of complicated CRIU Kernel integration. The
45

approach utilizes the constraint-based placement where new replicas are sent to the
healthy nodes using the start-first ordering to ensure that new containers are
operational before the old containers will terminate.

The migration workflow has seven steps, namely: query InfluxDB for healthy
target node (CPU < 60%, memory < 70%), add temp placement constraint
(node.id==target_node_id), service update with start-first ordering, Docker
Swarm creates new replica on target node, health checks verify new replica readiness,
load balancer adds new replica to routing pool, old replica receives SIGTERM and
drains connections during 30 second grace period before terminating. Finally,
SwarmGuard also eliminates temporary placement constraint and allows scheduling
to return to normal flexibility.

3.4 Development and Implementation
3.4.1 Development Methodology
SwarmGuard development followed the iterative approach passing through four
phases in 28 weeks. Phase 1 (Requirements Analysis and Literature Review, 8 weeks)
included a research survey, Docker Swarm analysis, limitation identification and
50

requirements establishment. Phase 2 (System Design and Architecture, 5 weeks)
resulted in monitoring framework specification, classification logic definition,
migration strategy design and technology stack selection.

Phase 3 (Implementation and Integration, 10 weeks) consisted of setup of cluster,
monitoring of stack-deployment, YAML configuration development, Python
automation script implementation, Docker Swarm API integration, and threshold
tuning. Phase 4 (Testing and Validation, 5 weeks) included stress application
development, experimental trial execution, metric collection, statistical analysis and
documentation.

3.4.2 Technology Stack Justification
InfluxDB is used as time-series database because of push-based ingestion which
allows sub-second metric availability for storage and visualization, and low resource
footprint for edge deployment. Threshold evaluation is not done by database queries
but by monitoring agents. Alternatives such as Prometheus (pull based, introduce
detection latency), TimescaleDB (SQL based complexity) and Graphite (Limited
querying) were considered, but rejected.

Python monitoring agents use docker-py SDK that provides native access to
Docker Stats API for metrics collection without container instrumentation. Event
driven Architecture with transmission of alerts via http using python requests library
allows for sub-100ms alert latency. Python Flask framework offers the endpoints with
the Recovery Manager for getting the alerts. The large ecosystem of Python language,
easy error handling using the python library (Asyncio) for concurrent operations and
faster development prompted the choice over Go (Tougher learning curve), Node.js
(Callback complexity) and Java (Heavyweight Runtime).

3.4.3 Cluster Infrastructure Setup
The 5-node Docker Swarm cluster has 1-manager, 4-worker topology offering fault
tolerant control plane via Raft consensus but separates the execution of workload to
dedicated workers. The master node performs cluster management such as scheduling
decision, service state monitoring, Raft consensus, and includes Python Flask
Recovery Manager. Worker nodes (worker-1 through worker-4) run application
workloads and run Python monitoring agents between production traffic and control
plane operations.

The physical network topology of the experimental Docker Swarm cluster is shown
in Figure 15. The architecture is 1 master node and 4 worker nodes (worker-1 to
worker-4) connected to each other through a 100Mbps Ethernet switch. InfluxDB and
Grafana are installed on a separate Raspberry Pi for centralized monitoring. Python
monitoring agents are executed on each of the worker nodes for the collection of
metrics distributed across the nodes. The Python Flask-based Recovery Manager runs
on the master node for centralized decision-making and orchestration of recovery.

Figure 15: Docker Swarm Cluster Network Topology
This separation avoids resource contention between SwarmGuard's
monitoring infrastructure and services being monitored by SwarmGuard. InfluxDB
and Grafana are installed on separate Raspberry Pi in order to separate the
observability overhead from the cluster resource measurements.

3.4.4 Monitoring Stack Deployment
InfluxDB has single replica deployment as Docker Swarm service restricted to
manager-1 with persistent volume mount to ensure data persistence across container
restarts. Database configuration, 2 GB memory is allocated for caching, the number
of metrics to retain is limited to 30 days (analyzing history vs. storage limitations),
and 10 seconds can be set for metric collection intervals for real-time monitoring to
match metric collection frequency.

53

Python monitoring agents are deployed as a single Docker Swarm service with
node-specific placement constraints (one agent per worker node). Each agent gathers
metrics from co-located containers using Docker socket (/var/run/docker.sock)
mounting, reading CPU percentage, memory usage and network I/O from Docker
Stats API. When thresholds go above for 2 consecutive intervals, agents send alerts
via http post back to Flask Recovery Manager with complete context of metrics.
Agents are also used to batch the metrics every 10 seconds and send them to InfluxDB
for historical analysis and Grafana visualization.

3.4.5 Automation Script Architecture
Python automation scripts are used to incorporate SwarmGuard's decision and
execution logic in five integrated modules. Alert Listener receives alerts from the
monitoring agents after evaluating thresholds that give threshold violation
notification. Scenario Classifier implements a network-aware decision logic that
processes CPU and network information in order to decide which one is Scenario 1
(migration) or Scenario 2 (scaling). Migration Executor interacts with Docker Swarm
API to know what healthy target nodes are, add placement constraints, start rolling
updates and track migration progress.

Scaling Executor handles the adjustment of the replica count with the cooldown
periods to avoid oscillation. Logger module: to record all the actions, decisions, and
results done, for historical analysis and debugging, they are being recorded into
InfluxDB.

3.4.6 Configuration Management
SwarmGuard uses YAML-based configuration files that allow tuning the
thresholds without changing the code. Configuration structure, including monitoring
parameters (collection interval, batch size), threshold values (CPU 75%, memory
80%, network boundaries), consecutive breach requirements (2 intervals minimum),
cooldown periods (60s scale-up, 180s scale-down), scaling factors (replica increment
count) and target node selection criteria (CPU < 60%, memory < 70%). Operators
54

make changes to parameters depending on the characteristics of the application, and
then restart automation scripts to apply changes without redeployment.

3.5 Research Methodology Overview
3.5.1 Experimental Design
Validation uses controlled experiments of reactive baseline recovery vs.
SwarmGuard proactive methods in two failure situations. Thirty total trials consist of
10 baseline reactive tests which setup Docker Swarm's default recovery performance,
10 Scenario 1 proactive tests which evaluate container stress migration effectiveness,
and 10 Scenario 2 proactive tests which assess traffic surge scaling responsiveness.
All experiments are done with the same cluster configuration, stress application, and
measurement procedures so that a valid comparison will be made.

Baseline reactive tests manually stop containers (docker kill), time until Docker
Swarm notices the failure via health checks, schedules replacement containers and
restores service availability. This establishes Mean Time To Recovery (MTTR)
detect-after-failure paradigm. Scenario 1 puts stress on the inject CPU with
computational workload but no increase of network traffic thereby activating the
migration logic of the SwarmGuard. Scenario 2 tests provide high network traffic at
the same time as CPU stress that will trigger horizontal scaling.

3.5.3 Evaluation Metrics
There are four main metrics that measure SwarmGuards effectiveness and
efficiency. Mean Time To Recovery (MTTR) the amount of seconds between the last
successful health check time before failure and the first successful health check time
after recovery, as a measure of overall recovery speed. Zero-Downtime Success Rate
is also a percentage of Scenario 1 trials that achieve complete migration without any
failed health checks during transition, where (successful migrations/total trials)x100%
is calculated. Above 80% is considered to be practically viable.

System Overhead measures the resource usage by the components of SwarmGuard.
CPU Overhead Measured as percentage of node CPU resources used by Python
monitoring agents, InfluxDB and Flask Recovery Manager when system is in steady
state. Network Overhead, it measures the network overhead in terms of bandwidth
used by metric batching and transmission of an alert over the network in Mbps for
each monitored service. Both metrics are used to validate conformance to efficiency
requirements (< 5% CPU, < 0.5Mbps network). Recovery Action Appropriateness
qualitatively evaluates the appropriateness of executed recovery actions to classified
scenarios and actual failure causes based on manual log analysis.

3.5.4 Statistical Analysis
MTTR measurements are statistically analyzed for the significance of measured
differences. Descriptive statistics are calculated or a mean, median, standard
deviation, minimum, maximum value for each trial group. Welch's t-test is used to
56

compare the mean MTTR between reactive baseline and proactive recovery in which
p-value < 0.05 threshold indicates statistical significance. Cohen's calculation of
effect size provides a measure of practical size of improvements where above 0.8 are
large effects and represent meaningful impact in the real world.

3.5.5 Experimental Procedure
Each experiment has standardized procedure to ensure reproducibility. First, check
for cluster health, ensure all nodes are operational, services are running and
monitoring is running. Second, make baseline measurements about pre-test CPU,
memory, and network measurements. Third, inject stress (appropriate endpoint (CPU
stress for Scenario 1, CPU + network for Scenario 2, manual kill for baseline)).
Fourth, observe SwarmGuard response or Docker Swarm default recovery based on
trial type.

Fifth, make detailed timing measurements such as stress initiation, alert generation,
recovery action and service restoration times. Sixth, allow and 5-minute cooldown
period between trials making sure metrics are stabilized. Seventh, export all metrics
& logs for off-line analysis.

Findings have important implications for platform design. Substantial MTTR

4.6.3 Limitations and Future Work
The 20% non-zero-downtime rate (Scenario 1) indicates proactive migration
doesn't guarantee zero downtime universally. Two failure cases reveal failures
occurred when load balancer synchronization delayed traffic routing or when target
nodes experienced resource contention. Future work could address through predictive
synchronization and more sophisticated target node selection.

Lease-based load balancer operates at application layer (Layer 7) introducing ~23ms additional latency versus kernel-level routing. For most applications this
overhead is negligible, but latency-sensitive applications might prefer kernel-level
routing. Future work could investigate eBPF-based implementation. Experimental
validation focused on single-service scenarios; real production runs multiple services
with complex dependencies requiring multi-service testing.

Rule-based classification achieved 100% accuracy in controlled testing but relies
on manually tuned thresholds (75% CPU, 80% memory, 35% and 65% network).
Different applications may require different thresholds. Future work could investigate

seconds to 0.6 seconds with 80% of migrations having zero downtime. Resource
overhead was still minimal (<5% CPU and <0.5 Mbps network traffic) meaning it can
be used in resource-constrained environments. These results prove the feasibility and
benefit of proactive recovery for both the technical and practical aspects.

5.2 Research Contributions
Research goes a step further and demonstrates the practical application of proactive
recovery principles to container orchestration; this goes beyond the autonomic
computing theory. Provides experimental results on achieving near-zero downtime in
84

containerized environments, resulting in a better understanding of fault tolerance in
distributed systems.

SwarmGuard is novel architecture for threshold-based monitoring plus contextaware decision making for Docker Swarm. Event driven design achieves less than one
second decision latency while having minimal resource overhead and addresses key gap
in existing container management systems. Custom lease-based load balancing
algorithm is intelligent traffic distribution without expensive continuous metrics
collection needs.

Research produces working implementation that can be deployed in production
environments where resources are limited. By ensuring compatibility with legacy
100Mbps infrastructure and having <5% computational overhead requirement,
SwarmGuard facilitates advanced fault tolerance to organizations with constricted
resources. Comprehensive evaluation framework sets the measurement of MTTR,
decision accuracy, network efficiency, and system overhead.

5.3 Implications for Practice and Theory
5.3.1 Practical Implications
SwarmGuard shows recovery of zero downtime requirement without costly
infrastructure upgrade. Minimal overhead (<5% CPU, <0.5Mbps network) allows it
to be feasible for SMEs with limited resources. Organizations can have proactive fault
tolerance with the use of commodity hardware and existing Docker Swarm
deployments. The 97% MTTR reduction is a direct translation into improved service
availability and user experience, which has the significant impact on SLA penalties
and customer churn.

85

5.3.2 Theoretical Implications
Research helps to validate the applicability of proactive recovery principles in
modern containerized systems, which helps to demonstrate that classical computing
principles are still relevant when translated to modern day cloud native architectures.
Successful implementation breaks with the assumption of reactive approaches for
container fault tolerance. Findings suggest threshold-based monitoring, if properly
tuned, has high prediction accuracy without the need for complex ML models, so
simpler approaches may be preferable in resource constrained environments.

5.4 Limitations of the Study
Testing done on 5-node cluster may not be representing large-scale deployments
with hundreds/thousands of nodes. Study concentrated on resource exhaustion and
container crashes; other failure modes (network partitions, cascading failures,
Byzantine faults) not heavily tested. Validation used standardized web application
workload, the workloads in real world applications with different characteristics may
exhibit different failure patterns affecting the prediction accuracy.

Testing assumed relatively stable network with controlled packet loss; highly
dynamic or unreliable networks may have effect on zero downtime migration capability;
The 80% zero downtime success rate is an indication that during the 20% of migrations,
there were brief downtime interruptions, so there is room to improve upon achieving
true guaranteed zero downtime recovery.

5.5 Recommendations for Future Work
Investigate hybrid methods using threshold-based detection and ML models
(LSTM, ensemble methods) to improve accuracy of predictions that capture complex
patterns of failures. Extend SwarmGuard to support hybrid and multi-cloud
environments, cross-providing migration. Port architecture to Kubernetes, the biggest
of the orchestration platforms, which requires platform-specific implementation.

86

Develop the mechanisms to deal with network partition failures with integration
with service mesh technologies or gossip protocols. Implement self-tuning mechanisms
that automatically change monitoring thresholds based on observed application
behavior, relieving much of the burden of manual configuration. Investigate security
implications of proactive migration such as possible attack vectors and authentication
issues during relocation.

Develop cost models for cloud deployment where proactive migration costs network
transfer. Optimization algorithms should take a balance between availability
improvements and increased operational expenses. Perform long-term stability testing
over weeks or months that exposes possible slow leaks from a resource or performance
degrading issues.

5.6 Final Remarks
This research shows how the proactive fault tolerance of containerized
systems is both sound in principle and quite feasible with existing technology.
SwarmGuard offers proof-of-concept to challenge prevailing reactive approaches and
demonstrate near zero downtime container orchestration with the help of intelligent
monitoring, context-aware decision making and graceful recovery mechanisms. The
findings have immediate practical value for organizations that want to improve
availability of service while conserving resources.
